Field guide · Music and audio

Music generation: from a style brief to audio you can release

You type “warm, cinematic, piano” and get a lovely 30 seconds. The ad needs 15 seconds, a vocal, a seamless loop and commercial clearance. Fix the deliverable first: a song, stems, a loop, a sound effect or a voice-over. Once that is fixed, tools and acceptance mean something.

For Anyone scoring a video, ad, game or podcast You leave with A music delivery sheet, a structure timeline and a path from generation to mixing Time 10 minutes to read; 2–4 hours for a 15-second ad cue Last checked 25 Nov 2026

This is Specul’s decision framework for music production, not a ranking of models or platforms, and not legal advice. Loudness, licences and platform rules remain with the original texts and official documentation.

If you only have five minutes
  1. Fix the deliverable: song, stems, loop, sound effect or voice-over.
  2. Turn style into parameters: instruments, tempo, mood, length and exclusions.
  3. Commercial use depends on four rights: training data, voice, samples and lyrics.

One concrete job

Take one concrete job: a 15-second coffee ad, light piano, loopable, no vocal, commercially cleared and loudness-matched to the platform. Put those five lines in the delivery sheet.

Write the deliverable first — finished track, stems, sound effect or voiceover — then fix structure, mixing and rights. A demo audio file is not a finished delivery.

1 · The deliverable decides the toolchain

“Make a song” can mean five different things: a streaming master, editable stems, a short-video loop, a game sound effect, or an ad voiceover. A master needs loudness and delivery; stems need separate instrument outputs, phase and naming; a loop must be seamless and tileable; a sound effect must be short, clean and loopable; a voiceover must match lip or duration. Without a deliverable, every later choice reworks.

Write a delivery sheet first: duration, sample rate, channels, format, loudness target, whether stems are needed, lyric language, rights scope and deadline. It constrains the generation tool and becomes the handoff to mixing, legal and the platform.

2 · Turn style into executable parameters

“Epic”, “healing” and “cyber” are not parameters. Break them into executable items: duration, number of sections, bars per section, tempo, time signature, key, instrumentation, voice type, language, emotional arc, reference tracks and forbidden elements. A generator may not understand all of them, but arranging, mixing and acceptance need them.

Use reference tracks to describe direction, not to request a copy. “Similar rhythmic density and instrumentation” is acceptable; a copyrighted melody, lyric or voice is not a template. The more specific the reference, the more important it is to record source and rights status, so “style reference” does not slide into “infringing sample”.

3 · Generation routes: text, MIDI, audio reference and singing

Text-to-music is good for quickly exploring instrumentation and mood, but weaker on exact bars, lyric alignment and editability. MIDI/DAW is good for fixing rhythm and structure before filling with instruments or generation. Audio-to-audio suits style transfer, continuation or variation, but inherits the source’s flaws and rights. Singing synthesis suits known lyrics and melody, but needs extra work on pronunciation, breath and emotion.

The usual order: fix structure and duration, decide which sections must stay editable, then choose the route. An ad song that will be revised bar by bar should start in MIDI/DAW with generated fills; one-off ambient music can start from text; a brand track with a named singer needs voice rights settled before generation.

Rule of thumb: if delivery is bar-accurate, put structure into MIDI/DAW first. Text-only generation is better for drafts and mood exploration than for a final master.

4 · Structure and lyrics: align to a timeline first

Music is time-based; structure must land on bars and seconds. Draw a timeline first: 0–8 s intro, 8–24 s verse, 24–40 s chorus; then align lyric lines to bars. Generators often drift at section boundaries, so either generate in segments and assemble, or align in a DAW afterwards. Do not expect one pass to land exactly.

Proofread lyrics separately: polyphones, names, numbers, rhyme, syllable count and language switches. Singing synthesis also needs stress, pauses and breath; otherwise every word is right and it still does not sound like a song. If a model wrote the lyrics, check facts and rights before recording or synthesis.

  • Write the timeline first: start/end seconds, bars, key and tempo for each section.
  • Align lyrics line by line with stress, pauses and breath.
  • Generate in segments: intro, verse, chorus and bridge separately, then assemble.
  • Keep dry vocals and accompaniment for remixing, language changes or lyric edits.

5 · Mixing and mastering: loudness, dynamics and stems

Before delivery, align at least three things: sample rate and bit depth (commonly 44.1 kHz or 48 kHz, 24-bit), channels and phase, loudness and true peak. Streaming platforms normalise loudness again; many use around -14 LUFS as a reference, but the exact target is platform-specific. Push the master too hot and the platform turns it down, often reducing dynamics.

Stem delivery needs naming, track order, gain and phase. Export each instrument separately; do not fake stems by duplicating a full mix with muted regions. Check for missing or duplicate tracks, sample-rate mismatch and DC offset. A little noise and breath often sounds more real than absolute cleanliness, but do not use noise to hide generation artefacts.

Sample rate/bit depthCheckConsistent chain, stated on deliveryTarget48 kHz generation, 44.1 kHz backingCommon error
LoudnessCheckFollow platform spec, leave true-peak headroomTargetPushing blindly to 0 dBFSCommon error

6 · Rights: training data, voice, samples and lyrics

A generated track involves four layers of rights: model and service terms, training data and style sources, rights in input material (reference audio, samples, lyrics), and the permitted use of output audio. They are not the same thing. A commercial model licence does not mean a reference track may be used for training or sampling; owning the output does not mean lyrics or voice likeness are free to use.

Voice is high-risk input. Using a real singer, voice actor or public figure needs consent, use, duration and a withdrawal mechanism. Using a platform voice library requires reading the permitted release channels. Model-written lyrics still need originality checks and fact review. Samples and covers involve separate recording and composition rights; changing a few notes does not bypass them.

Rule of thumb: write a rights table for whose voice, whose lyrics and whose reference track before generating. Material with unclear rights does not enter the chain.

7 · Cost: per finished minute, not per call

Generation calls are one cost line. Add retries, segmented generation, stem export, mixing and mastering, lyric proofreading, voice licensing, platform review and rework. Use “per finished track” or “per finished minute” as the denominator so the number is comparable with outsourcing, library music or in-house work.

A recomputable formula: cost per finished minute = (generation calls + retries + storage + licences) / finished minutes + mixing minutes × labour rate + rework. The denominator counts only accepted finished minutes, not every draft. If licensing or labour dominates, settle those before optimising model price.

8 · Failure branches: listen to the symptom, then locate the stage

Music failures are often not “the model cannot”. They sit in structure, alignment, mixing or rights. Work backwards from what you hear and do not change five parameters at once. Keep the raw generation, DAW project and master so before/after can be compared.

Sections wrong length
Suspect first: Generation drift, unlocked timeline
Check: Regenerate by bars or cut in DAW

Chorus does not land
Suspect first: No energy lift, repeated instrumentation
Check: Inspect instrumentation and dynamics per section

Lyrics wrong or swallowed
Suspect first: Polyphones, syllable count, stress markup
Check: Proofread text and phonemes line by line

Voice sounds robotic
Suspect first: Synthesis model, prosody, breath, mix
Check: Blind-listen dry vocal; change reference or model

Pops, clipping, phase cancellation
Suspect first: Gain staging, true peak, stereo processing
Check: Inspect waveform and mono fold-down

Platform rejects upload
Suspect first: Loudness, format, rights flags, metadata
Check: Check each platform delivery rule

Listen to the dry vocal, then the backing, then the master. Separating the three layers shows whether the problem is generation, performance or mixing.

9 · A minimum path to a finished track

Make a 30-second proof first to validate style, structure and voice, then extend to the full track. Settle the master and stems after the proof passes, so a full production does not reveal a wrong direction.

  • Write the delivery sheet: track, stems, loop, SFX or voiceover; duration, format, loudness, rights.
  • Write a one-page brief: structure, bars, tempo, key, instrumentation, voice, exclusions, references.
  • Make a 30-second proof: validate style and structure before generating the full track.
  • Choose the route: text, MIDI/DAW, audio reference or singing synthesis; mark editable sections.
  • Align lyrics and timeline: proofread line by line, mark stress, pauses and breath.
  • Mix and master: align sample rate, phase, loudness and true peak.
  • Build the rights table: model, references, samples, lyrics and voice, item by item.
  • Deliver master, stems, loudness report and rights record before uploading.
Done means structure lands, lyrics are correct, the mix is deliverable, rights are traceable and the platform accepts it — not merely that a song was generated.
