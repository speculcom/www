Field guide · 3D and digital humans

3D and digital humans: from concept art to an asset that imports

You have a 3D model that looks good. Do not deliver it yet. Import it into Unity and the scale may be wrong; rig it and the elbow collapses; put it on a phone and the frame rate halves. Three things decide whether it works: it imports, it animates, and it runs. Write where it will be used before deciding which steps AI can generate and which need a human finish.

For Anyone handing a 3D asset to a game, film, shop or digital-human project You leave with A deliverable-form table, an asset acceptance sheet and a path from concept to import Time 15 minutes to read; 1–3 days for one importable asset Last checked 25 Nov 2026

This is Specul’s production framework for 3D, not a ranking of models, engines or assets. Formats, specifications, licences and prices remain with the linked pages and original sources.

If you only have five minutes
  1. Fix the deliverable first: still, interactive model, shot asset or real-time avatar.
  2. Delivery means it imports into the target engine and plays at the target frame rate.
  3. Generation is only the start; topology, rigging, materials and export need a human finish.

The full guide starts below. Use the tables for reference; you do not need to memorise them.

One concrete job

Take one concrete job: a coffee machine in Unity for a 12-second product shot, 60 fps on a phone, with a lid-opening animation and a brand-colour light strip. Write the acceptance sheet for that delivery.

A deliverable imports into the target engine, runs at the target frame rate and passes the asset acceptance table. Generation only supplies the starting point.

1 · Four deliverables, four acceptance tables

“Make a 3D model” is not a delivery spec. A still needs silhouette, material and lighting. An interactive model must import into an engine, support collision and fit a frame budget. A shot needs motion, camera and continuity. A real-time avatar needs lip sync, expression, latency and long-run stability. Their asset structure, polygon budget and acceptance evidence differ completely.

Write the deliverable as one checkable sentence: on which device, in which engine, at which frame rate, viewed from how close, moving how far, accepted by whom. If that sentence cannot be written, the argument about “whether this model is usable” will only start at the end.

DeliverableMinimum assetAcceptance evidenceCommon mistake

Interactive modelDeliverableglTF or engine-native asset, collision, LODMinimum assetImports, selects, holds target frame rateAcceptance evidenceOnly turntable-ready in the modelling toolCommon mistake
Shot / shortDeliverableAnimated mesh, camera, timelineMinimum assetDeformation, motion, shot continuityAcceptance evidenceUsing a still to prove animation qualityCommon mistake
Real-time avatarDeliverableSkeleton, expression, visemes, audio chainMinimum assetLip sync, blink, latency, continuous runAcceptance evidenceUsing a demo clip to prove production readinessCommon mistake
Rule of thumb: without a deliverable spec, every later argument about “accuracy” is unverifiable.

2 · Where geometry comes from: generation, reconstruction, scanning, manual

Generative models are good at a first shape, not at guaranteeing topology, UVs, normals or part relationships. The usual failure is not “unrealistic” but an invented backside, fused parts, holes and colour baked into textures. Reconstruction and scanning capture a real object, but bring noise, missing surfaces and scale drift. Manual modelling is slowest, yet best for assets that must animate and be maintained.

Choose by “will this asset animate, enter an engine and be revised”, not by “which generator looks best”. Animated assets prioritise topology and rigging; single-image assets prioritise silhouette and material; real scenes prioritise scale and missing surfaces.

3 · Topology, UVs and normals: whether it can move and texture

A dense generated mesh is not a valid topology. Non-manifold edges, loose parts and badly placed poles collapse under deformation, break simulation and fail slicing for printing. Overlapping UVs contaminate textures; inconsistent texel density blurs close-ups and wastes resolution at distance; mismatched normals and tangents create hard edges and black faces in an engine.

Use a fixed order: scale and orientation, then non-manifold edges and loose parts, then polygon count and edge flow, finally UVs, texel density, normals and tangents. Reverse the order and you will retopologise a wrongly scaled asset for nothing.

4 · Materials and textures: fix the material model before resolution

A PBR material is not “more texture maps”. Base colour carries albedo, roughness and metallic drive reflection, normal carries surface detail, AO carries occlusion, emissive carries emission and opacity carries transparency. Colour maps are usually read as sRGB, data maps as linear; treating roughness as a colour map throws off reflection across the asset.

Resolution follows viewing distance and texel density, not “bigger is better”. A 4K map on a fingernail is waste; a 512 map across a wall is blur. Transparency, double-sided surfaces, refraction and decals also change sorting and performance; judge them in the target engine, not only in the modelling tool.

Base colour
What it controls: Albedo and pattern
Common error: Baking shadows and highlights into colour
Acceptance: Correct under neutral light

Roughness / metallic
What it controls: Reflection sharpness and metal response
Common error: Read as sRGB, wrong channel packing
Acceptance: Same material reads consistently under different lights

Normal / tangent
What it controls: Surface detail and tangent space
Common error: Inverted normals, inconsistent tangents
Acceptance: No hard edges or black faces in close-up

AO / emissive
What it controls: Occlusion and emission
Common error: Overdone AO, blooming emission
Acceptance: Layered shadows, clean emission

Opacity / decal
What it controls: Transparency, decals and sorting
Common error: Wrong transparent sorting, double-sided artefacts
Acceptance: Check from multiple angles in the target engine

More maps is not better. Fix the material model and texel density before deciding what to bake.

5 · Rigging and motion: weights cannot rescue bad topology

Rigging is not “add one skeleton”. A human needs shoulder, elbow, knee, spine and finger rotation axes; a face needs blendshapes or a bone set; a prop separates rigid and deformable parts; a scene separates static colliders from movable parts. Wrong joint placement, bone orientation or body proportions collapse under extreme poses no matter how carefully weights are painted.

Motion can be captured, keyframed, video-driven or retargeted. Each needs extreme-pose tests: arm raise, deep squat, torso twist, mouth open, eyes closed, fast head turn. A natural idle pose does not prove a rig.

Human character
Minimum rig: Spine, limbs, fingers, toes, eyes
Motion source: Keyframe, capture, retarget
Acceptance: Joints hold at extremes, feet do not slide

Face / avatar
Minimum rig: Jaw, lips, brows, lids, eyes
Motion source: Blendshapes, ARKit, audio drive
Acceptance: Lip sync, blink and expression do not overshoot

Quadruped / prop
Minimum rig: Spine, limbs, tail, movable parts
Motion source: Keyframe, capture, physics
Acceptance: No ground penetration, no detached parts

Scene / machine
Minimum rig: Hierarchy, pivots, constraints, collision
Motion source: Keyframes, blueprints or scripts
Acceptance: Correct opening, colliders follow

Rig failures usually start in topology and joint placement, not in weight painting.

6 · Rendering and real time: lock the device and frame rate first

Offline rendering trades time for quality; real time starts by fixing a frame budget. At 60 fps a frame is 16.7 ms, at 30 fps 33.3 ms and at 90 Hz XR 11.1 ms; that budget also pays for application logic, physics, animation, UI and audio. “It turns in the editor” does not prove the target device runs it.

Measure four numbers first: draw calls, triangles, texture memory and CPU/GPU time per frame. Then inspect overdraw, bone count for skinning, transparent materials and shader complexity. LOD is not a final optimisation; it is part of the asset spec, with polygon count, materials and switch distance written per level.

Desktop 60 fps
Budget to write: 16.7 ms per frame, keep 20–30% headroom
Measure first: CPU/GPU frame time, draw calls
Typical bottleneck: Shadows, transparency, post-processing

Mobile 30 fps
Budget to write: 33.3 ms per frame, heat and throttling
Measure first: Frame rate after ten minutes
Typical bottleneck: Texture bandwidth, skinning, overdraw

XR 90 Hz
Budget to write: 11.1 ms per frame, stereo
Measure first: Latency, reprojection, motion sickness
Typical bottleneck: Resolution, transparency, bone count

Web / WebGL
Budget to write: Download size, VRAM, first frame
Measure first: Load time, context loss
Typical bottleneck: Too many textures, shader compilation

Lock the target device and frame rate before arguing polygon count and textures. An editor turntable is not a real-time acceptance test.

7 · Digital humans: face, lip sync, eyes and long-run stability

Digital humans fail most often at the face. A good still can blink wrong, track eyes poorly, overshoot mouth shapes or make hair intersect the skin. Audio-driven lip sync must align phonemes, stress and pauses. A ten-second demo can be curated; ten minutes of speech exposes drift and fatigue.

Split the avatar into six acceptance layers: head, face, eyes, hair, body and voice. Head checks proportion and rotation; face checks expression range; eyes check gaze and blink; hair checks intersection and physics; body checks gesture and seated pose; voice checks lip sync, timbre and latency. Replacing any layer with “overall feel” causes rework at the client demo.

Head / proportion
What to build: Skull, features, neck-shoulder join
Acceptance: No intersection or deformation through rotation
Risk: Proportion breaks in close-up

Expression
What to build: Blendshape or bone set
Acceptance: Emotion and micro-expression do not overshoot
Risk: Mask-like expression

Eyes / blink
What to build: Gaze, pupil, lids
Acceptance: Follows camera, natural blink
Risk: Staring, mechanical blink

Hair / cloth
What to build: Hair strands, collision, cloth
Acceptance: No intersection on fast head turn
Risk: Intersects face, jitters

Lip sync / voice
What to build: Phonemes to visemes, stress, pauses
Acceptance: Long sentences stay aligned, latency measurable
Risk: Audio and video drift

Long run
What to build: Memory, heat, degradation
Acceptance: Thirty minutes without a crash
Risk: Slows down, VRAM leak

A digital human is easiest to fake at the face. Check lip sync, blink, head turn and long-run stability first.

8 · Export and import: the format can be right and the scene still wrong

glTF suits web and real time, USD suits film and scene assembly, FBX remains common for DCC exchange; each treats units, axes, materials, animation and skeletons differently. A successful export is not a successful import: scale can be off by 100, materials can drop, animation can be resampled and bone orientation can flip.

Accept in the target engine, not in the export dialog. After import, check scale, orientation, materials, animation, collision, LOD, naming and file size; keep a rollback-ready source asset and export record.

Units and scale
Why: DCC tools default to different units
How to verify: Place a known-size human or cube in an empty scene

Axes and facing
Why: Y-up / Z-up and forward axes differ
How to verify: View from four sides, check front and ground

Material conversion
Why: PBR channels may be simplified or dropped
How to verify: Compare each channel between source and target

Animation and frame rate
Why: Resampling changes timing
How to verify: Compare key poses and duration frame by frame

Collision and LOD
Why: Importers do not create them automatically
How to verify: Walk, collide and switch three LODs

Naming and manifest
Why: Assets get lost at scale
How to verify: Name by spec, attach source, textures and licence

Export is not the last step. Import into the target engine and run it before calling the export successful.

9 · Rights: model, training data, reference and person are four layers

A 3D asset carries at least four rights layers: the model and service terms, training data and style sources, input references (photos, scans, other models), and permitted use of the output. A commercial model licence does not make a reference photo commercial; owning the output file does not make a real-person scan usable as an avatar; a CC0 model can be modified, but trademarks, characters or buildings inside it are not automatically free.

Real-person scans, digital humans and voice clones also need consent, scope, duration, withdrawal and labelling. Keep consent, references, generation logs, edit records and publication scope in one asset manifest; without it, you cannot prove the basis for use later.

  • Give every asset one manifest row: source, author, licence, references, consent, generation tool, edits and publication scope.
  • Separate personal use, commercial publication, retraining and redistribution; do not treat one permission as all uses.
  • Check likeness, voice, name, trademark, building exterior and character separately; one “CC0” conclusion cannot cover them all.
  • Label synthetic media according to platform and applicable rules; keep a withdrawal and takedown path.
Rights are not one line saying “commercial use allowed”. Separate use, territory, duration, labelling and evidence.

10 · Cost: price accepted assets, not generation attempts

The common costing error is treating one generation as one finished asset. Real cost includes generation attempts, cleanup and retopology, UVs and textures, rigging and motion, rendering and transcoding, storage and versions, acceptance and rework. More generations do not make an asset cheaper; they may only postpone the rework.

Quote per accepted asset or accepted shot second, and write rework rounds, source-file delivery, exclusivity and later revisions into the contract. Per-token, per-image or per-run pricing cannot cover the manual stages.

Generation attempts
Unit: Per run / batch
Why it runs away: Endless sampling without a stop rule
Control: Set a batch cap and pass criteria first

Cleanup and retopology
Unit: Hours per asset
Why it runs away: Generated mesh cannot animate directly
Control: Fix polygon, edge-flow and UV targets

Rig and motion
Unit: Hours per rig
Why it runs away: New skeleton per asset, retarget fails
Control: Reuse skeletons and naming rules

Textures and materials
Unit: Maps per material
Why it runs away: Unbudgeted 4K/8K, wrong packing
Control: Set resolution from texel density

Render and transcode
Unit: Frame / GPU hour
Why it runs away: Oversampling, re-render after fixes
Control: Test at low resolution first

Storage and versions
Unit: GB per version
Why it runs away: Full copies per version, duplicate textures
Control: Incremental versions and retention

Acceptance and rework
Unit: Rounds per asset
Why it runs away: Unclear criteria, verbal changes
Control: Acceptance table plus written round notes

Quote per accepted asset, not per generation attempt.

11 · Failure branches: locate the stage before swapping tools

3D failures often surface after export, but the cause sits earlier. Locate the stage — geometry, material, rig, real time, export, rights — before deciding whether to fix the asset, the pipeline or the tool. Do not hide a scale or normal problem behind another generation.

Holes and floating parts
Check first: Non-manifold edges, connected components
Likely cause: Generation not cleaned
Fix: Fill, merge, retopologise

Wrong scale in engine
Check first: Units, axes, import settings
Likely cause: Source scene units differ
Fix: Unify units, recheck against reference

Collapses when bent
Check first: Joint placement, edge flow, weights
Likely cause: Topology unsuitable for deformation
Fix: Rebuild joint loops, then weights

Washed-out or bright material
Check first: Colour space, PBR channels
Likely cause: Wrong sRGB / linear assignment
Fix: Reset per channel, verify in engine

Frame drops
Check first: Draw calls, triangles, textures, skinning
Likely cause: No budget or missing LODs
Fix: Cut draw calls and textures first, then LOD

Lip-sync drift
Check first: Phonemes, visemes, frame rate, latency
Likely cause: Audio and video clocks differ
Fix: Align clocks, retest long sentences

Unclear rights
Check first: Model terms, references, consent
Likely cause: Generation logs without provenance
Fix: Complete the manifest, replace unusable assets

Locate the stage before swapping tools. A new tool does not fix scale, topology or rights.

12 · A minimum path: from delivery sentence to accepted asset

This path fits one asset that must enter an engine or appear in a shot. Each step leaves evidence before the next begins.

  • Write the delivery sentence: target device, engine, frame rate, viewing distance, motion range and acceptor.
  • Collect references and a rights manifest: photos, scans, model sources, licences, consent, trademark checks.
  • Choose the geometry source: generate, reconstruct, scan or model manually; start with a low-poly blockout.
  • Clean geometry: unify units, orientation and pivot, fill holes, remove loose parts, set the polygon budget.
  • Retopologise, unwrap, and fix normals and tangents around deformation and viewing distance.
  • Build materials and textures; accept each PBR channel, colour space and resolution budget.
  • Rig and animate: joints, weights, blendshapes, retargeting; run extreme-pose tests.
  • Export and import into the target engine: check scale, materials, animation, collision, LOD and frame rate.
  • Package the asset manifest: source files, textures, licences, consent, generation and edit records, acceptance table.
  • Deliver with a rollback: keep a reproducible version, record unresolved items and conditions for the next revision.
The minimum path is not the fastest path; it is the shortest reproducible, acceptable and rollback-ready loop.
