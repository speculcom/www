Field guide · Evaluation

Evaluation: what does this score actually tell you?

The leaderboard says 82, but half the answers are wrong in your business. The problem is usually not the model. The evaluation never answered your decision question: where the items came from, who scored them, whether the environment matched, and which failures were inspected. Write the decision first; choose the item set second.

For Anyone choosing between two models, versions or prompts You leave with A minimum re-runnable evaluation and a checklist for reading scores Time 12 minutes to read; about a day for the first evaluation Last checked 25 Nov 2026

This is Specul’s evaluation framework. It publishes no leaderboard and does not conclude which model is best. Item sets, scorers and raw results remain with the linked pages and sources.

If you only have five minutes
  1. A score only answers the question the test actually asked.
  2. Prevent leakage before scaling the item set.
  3. A correct final answer does not mean the agent’s process was correct.

The full guide starts below. Use the tables for reference; you do not need to memorise them.

One concrete job

Take one concrete job: choose between two support models. The question is not “which is smarter”, but “which can cut handoffs by five points in a week without getting refund policy wrong”. Turn that into the item set.

Write the decision question first, then choose the item set and metric. A score that cannot be reproduced is a lead, not a conclusion.

1 · Fix the decision question: whose decision does this score support

The same model needs different evidence for selection, acceptance, regression and external communication. Selection asks how to choose among candidates; acceptance asks whether a launch threshold is met; regression asks whether a change made things worse; communication asks for a one-line explanation that does not mislead. Fix the question before deciding what to measure.

Write the decision as one sentence: “Under task X and constraint Y, choose one of A/B/C, where the cost of error is Z.” That sentence determines item distribution, scorer and acceptable threshold. Without it, evaluation becomes “run every benchmark we can”.

DecisionEvidence neededNot needed
SelectionDecisionCandidate distribution on one item set, failures, costEvidence neededAn unrelated total scoreNot needed
AcceptanceDecisionThreshold, confidence interval, critical failure classesEvidence neededA cross-model rankingNot needed
RegressionDecisionBefore/after on the same item set, versions and configurationEvidence neededA new high score on a new item setNot needed
CommunicationDecisionWhat was measured, what was not, by whom, whenEvidence needed“Best” or “number one”Not needed

2 · Item set: prevent leakage before scaling

Bigger is not better. First check coverage of your task distribution: normal, edge, failure and adversarial inputs. Then check leakage: did the questions, answers or near variants appear in training data. A leaked item set cannot demonstrate generalisation, however high the score.

Version the item set. Every addition, removal or scoring change needs a version and changelog; otherwise before/after scores are not comparable. Public sets are useful external references; private sets are useful for real acceptance. Do not merge them into one total score.

Keep the items the model cannot do. A collection of only solved items creates a false high-scoring world. Failures, refusals and disputed items are the entry point for the next iteration.

3 · Scorer: who scores, and on what basis

There are four scorer types: exact match, rule checks, model judges and human review. Exact match is cheap but brittle; rules are explainable but narrow; model judges handle open-ended output but drift; human review is most reliable but costly. Choosing a scorer is a trade-off among cost, coverage and trust.

When using a model judge, fix the judge prompt, judge model, temperature and order, and sample its agreement with human judgement. Judges also favour long answers, polished formatting or outputs similar to their own style; place control samples in the set to expose these biases.

Scoring rules must explain why points were lost. A total score cannot guide improvement; it should locate factual errors, omissions, format, citations, permission violations or safety breaches. Scorer output should become the next iteration’s fix list.

Rule of thumb: if a scorer’s result cannot be explained or reproduced, it is an opinion, not an evaluation.

4 · Agent evaluation: a correct final answer does not mean a correct process

Agent evaluation must look at both outcome and trajectory: whether the task completed, steps were necessary, tool arguments were correct, permissions were exceeded, writes were duplicated and failures exited safely. An agent with a correct final answer that read data it should not have read has not passed.

Fix the environment: same tools, same data, same budget, same network conditions. Otherwise a “success rate” may just reflect environment differences. Record randomness too: run the same task repeatedly and see whether results are stable and failures cluster in certain input types.

Evaluate safety separately: prompt injection, unauthorised tool calls, data exfiltration, runaway cost, infinite loops. Do not substitute normal-task success rate for a safety conclusion; they measure different things.

5 · Reproduction: record model, version, environment and cost together

A reproducible evaluation records at least: model and version, quantization, runtime, hardware, item-set version, scorer version, temperature, max output, retry policy and run date. Miss one and others cannot tell why scores differ.

Cost is part of the result. On the same item set, a model that is more accurate but three times more expensive and five times slower may lead to a different decision. Record tokens, calls, tool fees, human review time and retries to make a real comparison.

Keep raw outputs, not just scores. Scores change with scorer versions; raw outputs can be re-judged under new rules later. Storing only scores locks the past.

6 · Reading a leaderboard: what is measured, then who measured it

Ask four questions when reading a leaderboard: what task was measured; is the item set public; who funded or ran it; does the score have a confidence interval. A number one without item set and scorer detail is advertising, not evidence.

Beware the total-score trap. Combining different capabilities into one number hides who chose the weights and why. Your task may use only one capability, yet the total drowns it. Reading capabilities separately is closer to a decision.

A leaderboard is a lead, not a conclusion. On a high score, return to the paper, item set and failure cases; on a low score, check for mismatched sets, different versions or unfair run configurations. Do not replace your own reproduction with a rank.

7 · Release gate: turn evaluation into an executable threshold

Evaluation must enter the release process, not stay in a report. Set a baseline first: current version’s score, cost, latency and safety-violation rate on a fixed item set. Then set gates: below what value release is blocked, and which critical failures are vetoes.

Gates should run automatically and allow human review. Automation ensures every change is checked against the same standard; human review handles disputed samples the scorer cannot judge. Automation alone or humans alone both leave blind spots.

Keep running after launch: sample real traffic and monitor failure classes and cost drift. Release is not the end of evaluation but the start of distribution change. Without post-launch regression evaluation, earlier scores expire quickly.

Rule of thumb: if an evaluation result cannot block a release, it is a weekly report, not a gate.

8 · Failure branches: when scores look good, suspect the item set first

The most common evaluation failure is not a weak model but easy items, answer leakage or scorer bias. When a score looks unusually high, check these three before celebrating.

SymptomCheck firstCommon fix
Unusually high scoreSymptomLeakage, easy items, scorer biasCheck firstUse a private set; add controlsCommon fix
High varianceSymptomSmall sample, temperature, environmentCheck firstExpand sample, fix configuration, repeatCommon fix
Judge disagreementSymptomJudge prompt, order, model biasCheck firstHuman audit, fixed order, calibrationCommon fix
Only averagesSymptomTail failures hidden by averagesCheck firstBreak down by class; list critical failuresCommon fix
Degrades after launchSymptomDistribution drift, cost/latency constraintsCheck firstSample online, alert on driftCommon fix

9 · A minimum path to a usable evaluation

Start with 30–50 of your own items; do not wait for a perfect set. A small set can expose problems if the items come from real tasks and failures are kept.

  • Write one decision question: what to choose, under what constraint, at what cost of error.
  • Sample from real tasks across normal, edge, failure and adversarial inputs.
  • Choose a scorer: exact, rules, model judge or human; state why.
  • Fix model, version, environment, temperature, retries and budget.
  • Keep raw outputs, scores, cost and failure classes.
  • Calibrate the scorer with human audits; record disagreements.
  • Put thresholds into the release process; critical failures veto.
  • Sample and rerun after launch; monitor distribution and cost drift.
Four common mistakes: treating a leaderboard as a conclusion; reporting only averages; judging only final answers, not trajectories; evaluation that cannot block a release.
