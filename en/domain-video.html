Field guide · AI video

AI video: from script to a finished clip you can deliver

You generate a stunning five-second shot. In the finished edit the shirt changes, the light jumps and the lip-sync drifts. Video delivery is judged on the whole clip: length, aspect ratio, story, shot continuity, sound, subtitles and rights. Write the delivery spec before choosing which shots to generate.

For Anyone making a short film, ad or talking-head video that has to ship You leave with A delivery spec, a shot list and a shot-by-shot repair path Time 12 minutes to read; 1–3 days for a 30-second piece Last checked 25 Nov 2026

This is Specul’s decision framework for AI video production, not a model leaderboard. Capabilities, prices and output licences remain with the linked pages and original sources.

If you only have five minutes
  1. Write the audience, placement, length and aspect ratio first.
  2. Generate shots, not the whole film.
  3. Budget by finished seconds, not by generation attempts.

The full guide starts below. Use the tables for reference; you do not need to memorise them.

One concrete job

Take one concrete job: a 30-second vertical launch video for a mobile feed, with voice, subtitles and one price line. Write those four things before choosing a model.

Fix the delivery spec and shot list first, then choose the generation method. A single demo clip is not a finished film.

1 · Write the delivery spec: who watches it, where and how

Before writing a prompt, put the delivery spec on one page: duration, aspect ratio, resolution, platform, captions, music, deadline and who approves. Different specs need different pipelines. A 15-second vertical clip and a three-minute horizontal film are not the same problem.

Also write what is unacceptable: wrong likeness, bad lip sync, garbled text, broken physics, missing brand elements, unclear music rights. Listing unacceptable outcomes in advance gives reviewers a shared language instead of everyone saying “it feels off”.

2 · Script and shot list: turn the idea into generatable shots

Write a one-sentence story, then break it into shots. Each shot carries one narrative job: establish a place, show an action, give a reaction, deliver a turn. A shot with three actions is hard for a generation model to keep coherent and leaves no alternate takes for editing.

A shot list needs at least: shot number, image, action, camera movement, duration, sound and reference. More style boards is not better. Each character, location and key prop should have one stable master reference plus a few angle references. Messier references make characters drift more.

Make keyframes before video. Keyframes are the cheapest confirmation point: composition, character, wardrobe, lighting and text placement can all be fixed there. Going straight to text-to-video bets composition, performance, camera and timing all at once; when it fails, you cannot tell what to change.

Rule of thumb: if the shot list cannot say what the audience should learn from a shot, that shot can probably be cut.

3 · Generation modes: text, image and editing each own a stage

Text-to-video is good for exploring concepts and mood but offers weak control. Image-to-video starts from an approved keyframe and preserves composition and character. Video editing changes parts of existing footage rather than generating from scratch. Chaining the three is more realistic than finding one model that does everything.

Do not choose a model from its demo reel alone. Check supported shot length, aspect ratios, audio, first/last frame, reference images, output resolution, commercial terms and watermarking. Demos show the best take, not the failure rate or retry cost.

Treat generation as sampling, not one-shot filmmaking. Generate several takes per shot, select by composition, action, stability and usable duration, then decide whether to regenerate or switch models. Accepting “choose one from many” is more controllable than chasing a perfect first take.

4 · Sound: audiences tolerate “wrong” picture less than wrong sound

A film’s credibility comes largely from sound. Treat voice, ambience, music and effects separately: voice must be intelligible, music must not overpower narration, effects must align with action. Build sound on the timeline before mixing.

For AI voice, check pronunciation, phrasing, emotion and lip sync. Short lines are more stable; generate in segments and assemble. For multilingual versions, re-listen rather than assuming subtitles equal dubbing. Music and SFX also need licence checks: commercial use, adaptation and platform distribution.

If the film has no dialogue, sound still cannot be skipped. Ambience, action sounds and music give images weight. A completely silent AI video easily feels like a slideshow.

5 · Editing and consistency: generate shots, not the whole film

Breaking the film into shots localises failure. If one shot breaks, redo only that segment; generate the whole film at once and a failure forces a full restart. Editing also handles rhythm, transitions, colour and captions, often more important than one more generation.

Consistency comes from three things: stable references, stable composition and stable descriptions. Wardrobe, hair, lighting and focal length belong in the shot list; do not swap synonyms in every prompt. Generation models are sensitive to wording changes; a consistent vocabulary reduces drift.

Fix output specs early: resolution, frame rate, colour space and caption-safe area. Changing aspect ratio in post recomposes the image and may crop key action. Compose for the final platform before generating.

6 · Cost: count approved seconds, not generation calls

The easiest video cost mistake is treating one generation as one finished second. Real cost includes multiple takes per shot, failed retries, upscaling, storage, voice, music, editing and review time. The denominator should be approved finished seconds, not generated seconds.

A usable formula: total cost = generation calls × cost per call + upscaling and storage + sound and music + editing labour + review and rework. Divide by approved finished seconds to get cost per finished second. Only then can you compare with outsourcing, live action or another tool.

Make a 15-second sample before committing to three minutes. The sample exposes character drift, lip sync, text, music and review disagreements; going straight to a long film multiplies rework.

7 · Rights and platforms: clear image, sound, fonts and likeness separately

AI generation does not mean no rights issues. Check separately: whether input assets may be used for generation; whether the model’s output terms allow commercial use; whether music, voice, fonts and brand elements have independent licences; whether real likeness or voice is used; whether the platform requires AI disclosure.

“The model says commercial use is allowed” does not mean “this film is cleared”. Model terms cover outputs, not input assets, music, likeness or platform rules. Keep evidence for four things separately: source, licence, scope and term.

Before publishing, run a checklist: input asset sources, output terms, music licence, font licence, likeness/voice permission, platform disclosure and watermark. If any item says “unknown”, do not publish yet.

Rule of thumb: if a film can prove only that the picture was AI-generated, not that sound, fonts, likeness and input assets are cleared, it is not a commercial deliverable.

8 · Failure branches: fix the shot, not the whole film

Video failures usually localise to a shot: character drift, broken action, garbled text, bad lip sync, physics errors, style mismatch. Decide whether it is a reference, prompt, model-capability or post issue, then redo that segment.

9 · A minimum path to production

Make a 15-second, three-shot sample with sound. If it passes review, extend to 60 seconds; do not start with a long film.

  • Write the delivery spec and unacceptable outcomes.
  • Write a one-sentence story and shot list; one job per shot.
  • Make keyframes; approve composition, character, wardrobe and lighting.
  • Choose the mode: text to explore, image to lock, editing to fix locally.
  • Generate multiple takes per shot; select by stability and usable duration.
  • Build the sound timeline before mixing and captions.
  • Cost by finished seconds; budget retries and rework.
  • Clear four rights classes, add platform disclosure, keep source assets for revisions.
Four common mistakes: prompts without a shot list; one generation as the whole film; ignoring sound and rights; treating a model demo as the delivery standard.
