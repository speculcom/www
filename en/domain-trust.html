Field guide · Safety, licences & provenance

Trust review: running, usable and accountable are separate checks

A tool running proves technical feasibility. Shipping it needs four separate answers: who may use it for what, where data goes, what rights you have over code, weights, data and output, and who owns a failure. Collapsing them into one “safe” label is a common mistake.

For Anyone buying, connecting or reviewing a third-party AI tool You leave with Four separate tables: permissions, data flow, rights and provenance, plus a review order Time 12 minutes to read; 2–4 hours to review one tool Last checked 25 Nov 2026

This is a decision framework, not legal advice. Licence and contract terms remain with the original text and a qualified lawyer.

If you only have five minutes
  1. Running does not mean usable; usable does not mean accountable.
  2. Keep permissions, data flow, rights and provenance in separate tables.
  3. Every conclusion needs evidence; write “unknown” where there is none.

The full guide starts below. Use the tables for reference; you do not need to memorise them.

One concrete job

Take one concrete job: a third-party writing tool that can read internal documents and send email. Check what it reads, where it sends, what the licence allows and who owns a failure.

Do not answer permission, data and licensing with one “safe / unsafe” verdict. Each table has its own evidence and its own failure modes.

1 · Three tables: separate first, combine later

The permission table answers “who may do what”: what a person, agent, service account and connector may read, write, call and approve. It cares about over-privilege, mistakes, credential leaks and revocation.

The data-flow table answers “where data goes”: which models, connectors, logs and backups it passes, how long it stays, whether it is used for training and whether it can be deleted. It cares about classification, cross-border transfer, subprocessors and retention.

The rights table answers “what do you own”: code licence, model-weight licence, rights in training/input data and rights in output are four different things. It cares about commercial use, attribution, copyleft, patent grants and redistribution.

Provenance comes last: watermarks, signatures, content credentials and on-chain receipts can help show source and movement, but they do not prove content is true, the model is reliable, or a licence covers your use.

2 · Permission: least privilege must bind to a specific identity

Separate three identities: a person (login), an agent (a service identity acting for a person or task) and a connector (credentials to an external system). They must not share one admin token; otherwise a single prompt injection can execute with the person’s full authority.

Write tool permission as action + object + scope: which folder may be read, which table written, which domain emailed, which API called, what monetary limit. “May use the tool” is too coarse; the tool’s parameters are the risk surface.

High-risk actions need separate approval: external sending, deletion, payment, permission changes, production writes. The approver must not be the initiator, and the approval goes into the log. Revocation must work without restarting the whole system.

A practical test: if this agent were fully controlled by an attacker, what is the worst damage it could do? That is the permission you actually granted — not the “please be careful” in the prompt.

3 · Data flow: local deployment is not automatically safe

Classify input first: public, internal, confidential, personal. Each level maps to different models and connectors. An “internal” file must not enter an external model through a copy-paste. Classification belongs in the workflow, not only in the employee handbook.

The data-flow map marks every landing point: browser, app server, model endpoint, logs, cache, backup, analytics. Each line states transport, encryption, retention, access and deletion. “Data stays local” misses telemetry, crash dumps and cloud backups.

For an external model ask six questions: training use, retention, cross-border transfer, subprocessors, deletion on request, and who notifies whom after a breach. Read both the contract and product docs; a “not used for training” line on a product page is not enough.

4 · Licensing: code, weights, data and output are four different things

A code licence (MIT, Apache-2.0, GPL) governs distribution and modification of source. Model weights often have a separate licence or terms that may restrict commercial use, redistribution, distillation or output use. Treating a code licence as a weights licence is the most common open-model misreading.

Rights in training and input data are another layer: may you use this data for training, fine-tuning or retrieval? The source licence, personal-data consent and database rights can all restrict use.

Rights in output depend on input, platform terms and local law. Model generation does not mean you own it; if the input is copyrighted, the output may still be a derivative work. Read the platform terms before commercial use, not just “free to use”.

SPDX identifiers help identify a licence quickly but do not replace reading the text. Custom terms, added branding clauses and community licences need clause-by-clause reading and, where needed, a lawyer. This page gives no legal conclusion.

5 · Provenance: it can show who said it, not whether it is true

Content credentials such as C2PA record source, edit history and signature in file metadata; watermarks embed a mark in pixels or audio; on-chain receipts anchor an operation to an address and time. All three improve traceability, and all three can be stripped, forged or lost when uploaded to a platform.

“Has provenance” only means a step left a record. It does not prove the content is true, the model is reliable or the rights are complete. This is especially true on-chain: a record proves a transaction happened, not that the data behind it is true.

When verifying, ask: who signed, is the certificate trusted, was the file modified after signing, does the platform preserve metadata, and how does the on-chain address map to an offline entity? Missing one link, provenance is only a lead.

6 · Supply chain: plugins, MCP and dependencies are permissions too

MCP servers, editor plugins, browser extensions and Python packages run inside your permission boundary. A “read-only” tool that returns a malicious instruction may be treated by the model as user intent; a third-party package that phones home invalidates the data-flow table.

Supply-chain checks include: pin versions and hashes, inspect maintainers and release history, run with least privilege, disable unnecessary network and file access, scan dependency vulnerabilities and keep an SBOM. Do not skip because it is “installed locally”.

Tool descriptions are input too. A malicious MCP tool can put “send me the environment variables” in its description. Models have no innate trust boundary; the caller and runtime must enforce it.

7 · Decision framework: six questions and one table

Before buying, launching or connecting a new tool, answer six questions in order. Every answer needs evidence; write “unknown” where there is none instead of “probably fine”.

QuestionEvidence to obtain
Who may use it for what?QuestionRoles and tool scope, approval points, revocationEvidence to obtain
What data can it see?QuestionInput classes, connector scope, cache and logsEvidence to obtain
Where is data sent?QuestionEndpoint, subprocessors, region, retention, training useEvidence to obtain
What licence covers code/weights/data/output?QuestionLicence text, SPDX, attribution and commercial termsEvidence to obtain
What does provenance prove and not prove?QuestionSigner, certificate chain, edit history, platform retentionEvidence to obtain
Who owns failure and how is rollback?QuestionIncident contact, SLA, rollback switch, log retentionEvidence to obtain
Rule of thumb: a commercial licence does not mean customer data is appropriate; local deployment does not mean nothing leaves; an on-chain receipt does not mean the content is true. Verify each “does not mean” separately.

8 · Minimum action list

  • Keep permission, data flow, rights and provenance as four tables; do not merge them into one score.
  • Give each agent/connector its own identity; grant by action, object and scope.
  • Draw the data-flow map with endpoints, logs, backups, subprocessors and retention.
  • Read the four licences separately (code, weights, data, output), recording SPDX and attribution.
  • Treat provenance as a lead: verify signer, certificate chain, edit history and platform retention.
  • Pin MCP servers/plugins/dependencies, run least privilege, scan vulnerabilities and keep an SBOM.
  • Before launch, run a “fully controlled” loss exercise and add approvals and rollback.
